Abstract—Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS) progressively
eliminates voluntary muscle control while typically sparing eye
movement, making gaze-based text entry one of the few remain-
ing communication channels for patients in advanced stages of the
disease. Existing eye-gaze augmentative and alternative commu-
nication (AAC) systems are often slow, expensive, and dependent
on dedicated infrared hardware. This paper presents GazeSpeak,
an open-source, webcam-only assistive eye gaze typing tool that
combines MediaPipe-based iris tracking, three-point horizontal
calibration, a step-based gaze-navigation scheme, large language
model (LLM)-powered word prediction and abbreviation expan-
sion, an LLM-driven binary-choice conversation engine, rapid-
blink emergency alerting with SMS notification, and text-to-
speech output. The system runs on consumer hardware using
a single webcam and requires no infrared tracker. We describe
the architecture, implementation, and design rationale of each
module, and situate the work within recent literature on LLM-
accelerated gaze typing, calibration-free interfaces, and brain-
computer interface typing paradigms for ALS. GazeSpeak is
implemented in Python using PyQt6, OpenCV , and MediaPipe,
with Groq-hosted Llama 3.3 70B providing LLM capabilities.
The system is designed to operate across multiple stages of ALS
progression through its modular, multi-modal input architecture.
Index Terms—Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis, Eye Gaze Typ-
ing, Augmentative and Alternative Communication, Large Lan-
guage Models, Assistive Technology, MediaPipe, Webcam-Based
Tracking.
I. I NTRODUCTION
Amyotrophic Lateral Sclerosis (ALS) is a progressive neu-
rodegenerative disease that destroys motor neurons, gradually
stripping patients of the ability to speak, move their limbs,
and eventually breathe independently [3], [9]. Because the
oculomotor pathways are typically preserved even in advanced
disease stages, eye gaze has become the primary input modal-
ity for augmentative and alternative communication (AAC)
systems used by this population [1], [6].
Despite decades of research, gaze typing remains slow.
On-screen keyboards driven by eye trackers typically support
fewer than ten words per minute, roughly an order of magni-
tude below conversational speech [6]. This gap discourages
extended interaction and contributes to the social isolation
commonly reported among ALS patients [8]. Two additional
obstacles compound the problem. First, many gaze-tracking
systems depend on dedicated infrared hardware and lengthy
calibration procedures that are expensive, difficult for patients
with restricted head control, and prone to drift [2], [5], [14].
Second, research on typing acceleration, low-cost tracking,
brain-computer interfaces (BCI), and voice preservation has
advanced along mostly separate paths, with comparatively
little work integrating these advances into a single deployable
tool tailored to ALS [1].
Three specific advances motivate this work. LLM-powered
interfaces have demonstrated 29–60% improvements in text-
entry rate over baseline keyboards and 57% savings in
motor actions [1]. Blink-coded alphabet trees have enabled
calibration-free typing on ordinary webcams [2]. Webcam-
based deep-learning facial landmark trackers have removed the
dependence on infrared hardware entirely [5]. BCI paradigms
offer a fallback modality for patients who progress to a fully
locked-in state [3], [11].
This paper presents GazeSpeak, an open-source assistive eye
gaze typing system that integrates several of these advances

into a working, deployable application. The system runs on
consumer hardware with a single webcam, uses MediaPipe
Face Mesh for iris tracking, provides LLM-accelerated word
prediction and abbreviation expansion through Groq-hosted
Llama 3.3 70B, supports a binary-choice conversation mode
for patients who find letter-by-letter typing too slow, and
includes a rapid-blink emergency alert system with SMS
notification via Twilio. The specific contributions are:
1) A complete, open-source implementation of a webcam-
only gaze typing system that requires no infrared hard-
ware or commercial eye tracker.
2) A three-point horizontal calibration scheme with IQR-
based outlier rejection that adapts gaze zone thresholds
to each patient’s eye movement range.
3) A step-based gaze navigation model with hysteresis-
filtered zone transitions, where horizontal eye direction
acts as discrete left/right arrow keys rather than contin-
uous cursor control.
4) Integration of LLM-based word prediction, context-
aware abbreviation expansion, and a binary-choice con-
versation engine that reduces the motor effort required
for communication.
5) A rapid-blink emergency alert mechanism using Eye
Aspect Ratio (EAR) blink detection, audible alarm, and
Twilio SMS notification.
II. R ELATED WORK
A. LLM-accelerated gaze typing
Cai et al. developed SpeakFaster, an LLM-powered interface
for abbreviated text entry that saved 57% more motor actions
than traditional predictive keyboards in offline simulation and
achieved text-entry rates 29–60% above baseline in lab and
field testing with two eye-gaze AAC users with ALS [1].
A companion field study instrumented the everyday gaze-
typed communication of an ALS patient over an extended
period to characterize real-world conversational patterns [6].
Car`ıa surveyed the convergence of BCI spellers with LLMs,
tracing the evolution from early statistical language mod-
els to contemporary transformer-based predictive writing [7].
Mirbagheri and Chau presented EyeChat, a gaze-controlled
web-browsing and chatbot interface for an augmented-reality
display, reporting 9.88 words per minute with an LLM-driven
response generation system [16].
B. Low-cost webcam and blink-based interfaces
Lee and Lee proposed a blink-to-alphabet tree that narrows
a 26-letter alphabet through three sequential blink-coded deci-
sions and integrates an LLM API for error correction, outper-
forming three baseline methods in speed and accuracy without
calibration [2]. Karatay et al. developed a webcam-only, deep-
learning-based facial landmark tracker that eliminates the need
for blinking, enabling use by patients who can no longer blink
voluntarily [5]. Molina-Cantero et al. reviewed visible-light,
low-cost camera eye-tracking methods, cataloguing calibration
strategies, head-pose invariance techniques, and the persistent
trade-off between affordability and tracking accuracy [14].
C. Brain-computer interface typing
Li et al. conducted a PRISMA-guided review of 23 studies
applying BCI paradigms to ALS communication, concluding
that recent multidisciplinary efforts have improved decoding
precision but that user variability, BCI illiteracy, and fatigue
remain open limitations [3]. Peters et al. introduced Inquiry
Preview, a multimodal extension of the RSVP-Keyboard that
fuses switch input with EEG and language-model evidence; a
potential end user with incomplete locked-in syndrome typed
fastest with the combined modality [11]. An NIH-funded
initiative on a rapid neural typing interface for veterans with
ALS reflects continued investment in translating BCI-based
typing toward deployment [15].
D. Datasets, hardware, and broader communication support
Ngo et al. released EEGET-ALS, a public EEG and eye-
tracking dataset from six ALS patients and 170 healthy con-
trols performing spelling tasks [4]. Liu et al. demonstrated
a wireless MXene-based smart contact lens that encodes
blink patterns into a resonance-frequency signal, validating
biocompatibility in vivo [13]. On the communication support
side, Garbey et al. developed AI tools combining pulse and
speech analysis to detect emotional responsiveness during ALS
clinic visits [8]. Regondi et al. combined voice banking with
AI voice-cloning to preserve a patient’s vocal identity for
AAC devices [9]. Barton et al. surveyed AAC specialists
on tele-AAC versus in-person evaluations for ALS patients
[10]. A medRxiv study proposed an AI-driven approach to
augmentative and assistive communication for ALS patients
[12].
III. S YSTEM ARCHITECTURE
GazeSpeak is a desktop application built in Python. The
system is organized into five modules: gaze tracking, user
interface, text prediction, emergency alerts, and audio. Fig. 1
illustrates the data flow between these modules. The appli-
cation is built on PyQt6 for the GUI, OpenCV for webcam
capture, MediaPipe Face Mesh for facial landmark detection,
and Groq-hosted Llama 3.3 70B for LLM capabilities.
A. Gaze tracking
The gaze tracker runs as a background QThread, capturing
webcam frames at 640 ×480 resolution and processing each
frame through MediaPipe’s Face Landmarker task. The model
detects 478 facial landmarks per frame, including five iris land-
marks per eye (indices 468–477). Gaze direction is computed
as the horizontal and vertical iris ratio: the position of each iris
center relative to the corresponding eye corners, normalized
to [0, 1]. The ratios from both eyes are averaged to produce a
single gaze coordinate.
A confidence metric is computed from the agreement be-
tween the two eyes. When iris landmarks are unavailable (e.g.,
due to occlusion), a fallback method estimates gaze from
eye corner midpoints at reduced confidence. An exponential
moving average (EMA) filter smooths the gaze signal before
it reaches the navigation controller. The smoothing factor is

user-configurable, defaulting to 0.25, which balances respon-
siveness against jitter.
B. Three-point horizontal calibration
On each application launch, a full-screen calibration se-
quence records the patient’s gaze ratio at three horizontal posi-
tions: fully left, straight ahead (center), and fully right. At each
position, the system collects 60 gaze samples (approximately
two seconds at 30 fps), rejects outliers using IQR filtering
(samples outside Q1−1.5·IQR to Q3+1.5·IQR are discarded),
and takes the mean of the remaining samples.
The three recorded values ( xleft, xcenter, xright) are used
to define zone boundaries. The calibrated screen position

The asymmetric margins and minimum floors ensure both

the patient’s neutral gaze falls. Calibration data is validated:
the spread between left and right must exceed 0.05 in raw gaze
units, and the center value must lie strictly between them. If
validation fails, the patient is asked to retry.
C. Step-based gaze navigation with hysteresis
Rather than mapping gaze position to a continuous screen
cursor, GazeSpeak uses a three-zone, step-based navigation
model. Looking left of zleft triggers a single leftward step of
the highlight. Looking right of zright triggers a single rightward
step. Returning to the center zone (between the two thresholds)
stops navigation and arms dwell-time selection: if the patient’s
gaze remains centered for a configurable dwell period (default
1200 ms), the currently highlighted key is selected.
To prevent false triggers from noisy gaze data, zone tran-
sitions require confirmation over multiple consecutive frames
(hysteresis). A zone change is only committed after two con-
secutive frames (at 30 fps, approximately 66 ms) indicate the
same new zone. This filters out transient spikes without adding
perceptible delay. The navigation highlight wraps circularly
through prediction suggestions and keyboard rows. When the
highlight moves past the last key in the last keyboard row, it
wraps to the first prediction slot, and vice versa.
D. On-screen keyboard and dwell selection
The keyboard is a QWERTY layout augmented with special
keys: SPACE, BACKSPACE, SPEAK (triggers TTS), CLEAR,
PHRASES (switches to the quick-phrases panel), and SET-
TINGS. Key selection occurs through dwell: when the patient
returns gaze to the center zone after navigating to the desired
key, a countdown timer begins. A visual progress ring fills
around the highlighted key. If gaze remains centered for the
full dwell duration, the key is pressed. Any zone transition
cancels the timer.
E. Word prediction
GazeSpeak combines two prediction paths running in par-
allel. Local prefix matching: a frequency-ranked dictionary
(approximately 300 entries weighted toward medical, comfort,
and daily-need vocabulary) provides instant completions as the
patient types. Results are sorted by frequency and filtered to
exclude exact matches with the current prefix. LLM context-
aware prediction: in the background, the current sentence and
partial word are sent to Groq-hosted Llama 3.3 70B via the
Groq API. The model is prompted to return a JSON array
of the five most likely word completions, prioritizing ALS
patient communication context (medical needs, comfort, social
interaction). When the LLM response arrives, it replaces the
local predictions in the UI. Temperature is set to 0.3 for
focused predictions.
The two-tier approach ensures the patient always sees sug-
gestions instantly (local), while benefiting from contextually
smarter predictions when the LLM response returns (typically
within 200–500 ms).
F . Context-aware abbreviation expansion
For patients who prefer to type shorthand, the system detects
likely abbreviations using a set of heuristics: the input is
short (≤5 characters), contains few or no vowels relative to
consonants, and does not match a list of common English short
words. When an abbreviation is detected, the system sends it
along with the full conversation history (caretaker questions
and prior patient responses) to Llama 3.3 70B, which returns
2–4 plausible expansions ranked by confidence.
For example, if the caretaker asks “Where did the sound
come from?” and the patient types “ftk”, the system might
suggest “from the kitchen”, “from the kids”, or “from the
terrace”. Each letter in the abbreviation is treated as poten-
tially the first letter of a word. The LLM response includes
confidence scores and reasoning for each expansion, and the
suggestions are ranked by confidence before display.
G. Binary-choice conversation engine
For situations where letter-by-letter typing is too slow or
fatiguing, GazeSpeak provides a conversation mode. When the
caretaker types or speaks a question (via the context bar at the
bottom of the screen), the system generates exactly two answer
options using Llama 3.3 70B. The options are displayed as two
large cards: one on the left side and one on the right side of
the screen.
The patient selects an option by looking in the correspond-
ing direction (left or right gaze). After each selection, the
engine generates a follow-up pair that narrows the patient’s
intended meaning. After up to five rounds of binary nar-
rowing, the LLM composes a natural spoken sentence from
the patient’s selection trail and speaks it aloud via TTS. For
=== PAGE 4 ===
example, if the caretaker asks “How are you feeling?”, the
patient might narrow through “Not great” → “I have pain”→
“In my head” across three rounds, and the system speaks the
composed sentence “I’m not great. I have pain in my head.”
This mode requires only three gaze gestures to compose
a full response, compared to dozens of individual key selec-
tions for letter-by-letter typing. The patient can also request
regeneration of options, undo a previous selection, or switch
to keyboard mode at any point.
H. Rapid-blink emergency alert
The gaze tracker continuously computes the Eye Aspect
Ratio (EAR) for both eyes using six landmark points per eye,
as shown in Eq. (4):

where p1–p6 are the outer corner, upper lid (two points), inner
corner, and lower lid (two points) respectively. A blink is
registered when the average EAR across both eyes drops below
0.21 for at least two consecutive frames and then rises above
the threshold (i.e., the eyes reopen).
The BlinkAlertManager maintains a sliding window of blink
timestamps. If five or more blinks occur within a four-second
window, the system triggers an emergency alert: (1) a full-
screen red overlay covers the application; (2) a continuous
siren alarm plays through the system speaker (alternating fre-
quency sweeps between 800–1200 Hz); (3) an SMS is sent to
the configured caregiver phone number via the Twilio API. A
60-second cooldown prevents repeat triggers. Blink detection
is automatically paused during calibration and resumes with a
two-second grace period to avoid false positives from residual
calibration blinks.
I. Caregiver mode and speech-to-text input
The caregiver interacts through a context bar at the bottom
of the screen. The caregiver can type a question directly
via the physical keyboard or use speech-to-text (AssemblyAI
real-time streaming API) to dictate their question verbally.
The transcribed or typed question is then fed to both the
abbreviation expander (as context) and the conversation engine
(as the prompt for binary-choice generation). The application
also supports full keyboard and mouse input for caregiver-side
configuration, settings adjustment, and recalibration.
J. Text-to-speech output
Composed sentences, quick phrases, and conversation-mode
responses are spoken aloud using the pyttsx3 offline TTS
engine. Speech rate is user-configurable through the settings
panel.

B. Design decisions
Several design choices warrant discussion.
Step-based versus continuous cursor. Continuous gaze-to-
cursor mapping is the approach used by most commercial
eye trackers [14]. We chose step-based navigation because
webcam-only iris tracking lacks the spatial precision of in-
frared systems. By discretizing gaze into three zones, the
system tolerates tracking noise that would make continuous
cursor control impractical. The trade-off is slower maximum
throughput, since each key requires at least one zone transition.
Hysteresis filtering. A two-frame hysteresis requirement
(approximately 66 ms at 30 fps) prevents false zone transitions
caused by momentary tracking noise, without adding percepti-
ble delay. This is simpler than the Kalman filtering sometimes
used in gaze tracking [14] and sufficient for the three-zone
model.
Asymmetric zone thresholds. The calibration-derived thresh-
olds use different margin ratios for the left (55%) and right
(45%) sides, with minimum floors of 4% and 12% respectively.
This accounts for the observation that most users have slightly
asymmetric gaze ranges, and ensures both zones are reachable
even with a skewed neutral gaze position.
LLM model choice. We use Llama 3.3 70B via the Groq API
because it provides sub-second inference latency with reason-
able cost, making it practical for real-time typing assistance.
The system degrades gracefully without internet: local prefix
matching continues to function.
C. Limitations
GazeSpeak has not yet been evaluated in a formal user study
with ALS patients. The system requires a three-point calibra-
tion on each launch, which may be difficult for patients with
very restricted eye movement range. The binary conversation
engine depends on the LLM generating appropriate and non-
harmful options, which is not guaranteed. The emergency blink
alert uses a fixed threshold (five blinks in four seconds) that
may need per-patient tuning to avoid false positives in patients
with abnormal blink patterns. The step-based navigation model
trades typing speed for robustness to tracking noise, and the
system’s throughput has not been formally measured against
commercial eye trackers.
VI. C ONCLUSION
GazeSpeak combines four things that have mostly been
studied separately in prior work — webcam-only iris track-
=== PAGE 6 ===
ing, LLM-accelerated prediction, a binary-choice conversation
mode, and blink-triggered emergency alerting — into a single
deployable tool that a patient can run on ordinary consumer
hardware. None of its individual components is entirely new:
LLM-accelerated typing, calibration-free interfaces, and BCI
fallback paradigms have each been demonstrated before [1]–
[3], [11]. What has been missing, and what this paper con-
tributes, is the integration: a system a caregiver could actually
install and use today, without infrared hardware or a research
lab.
That gap between “demonstrated in the literature” and
“usable by a patient” is also where the open questions sit.
The three-point calibration, the fixed blink-alert threshold, and
the step-based navigation model were all built as reasonable
defaults rather than values tuned against real patients, and the
paper is explicit about that in the limitations above. The next
step is a controlled user study with ALS patients to measure
typing speed, error rate, and user satisfaction against those
defaults, followed by work on a BCI fallback channel for
patients who progress to a locked-in state.
